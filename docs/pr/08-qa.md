# Q&A prep

*Answers for the 3-minute Q&A, short first, with backup. Part of the pitch docs; start at [README.md](README.md).*

The Q&A is 3 minutes, so the first answer to each question is short; the rest is backup. Status is as of commit `6b1cc85`.

## The hardest questions first

These came from a critique of the earlier draft, plus the IP and creator questions that follow from Karthik's decisions. Rehearse these.

**1. "You say *any* game. I'll name one now: what happens when Muse can't build it? What share of novel ideas actually play as intended, and who judged?"** Try it: the "How the game works" card will tell you which parts are new to Muse before it builds. On our 12-request eval suite, all 12 became games that passed our gate, with 88% of the asked-for features present (D30). Muse judged those itself, the judge swings by about 10 points, and franchise themes scored poorly before our fix. Calm and puzzle games are blocked today because the gate expects a run you can lose. A review found 9 of 10 hard ideas (Wordle-style, Connections-style and others) fit the format, but we haven't built those 9 yet (D32). So: most ideas, honestly flagged, not every idea.

**2. "Doesn't the anatomy back-and-forth break 'one sentence, 30 seconds'? How many turns does a typical idea take?"** It adds one card, drafted in 4 to 7 s (D33). Muse asks only about parts it guessed and isn't sure of, so for most requests you read it and tap Make. We haven't measured turns per person. The bigger honest number is the build: a game made from scratch takes a median 99 s, a ready formula 28 s. "30 seconds" is only true for the ready formulas.

**3. "You'll make a game with Star Wars or Mario by name? Who carries the IP risk at Meta scale, and how does that square with 'original games only'?"** See [where the IP line sits](#where-the-ip-line-sits) below. Short version: a theme a person asks for is honored by name in *their* game; our own seeded content stays original; no specific game's levels or art are ever copied; and at Meta scale, a franchise request would go through the same rights and policy checks as any user content, which we haven't built.

**4. "Your board's 'strangers' are bots, and you have no real-user evidence. Why should I believe someone who loses to a stranger sends a friend request instead of closing the app?"** You shouldn't yet, and we say so on stage: Maya is a demo person, and Add friend goes to a bot row. What we have is direction from research (rivals who affect each other form ties [72]; people underestimate how much others like being contacted [69]) and a test we'd run first: the hallway test and a Group-level pilot with "first new friend within 14 days" as the north star ([06](06-closer-together-and-engagement.md#how-wed-know-it-works)).

**5. "Your backend now makes the Muse calls. Where do my Group's posts go, who sees them, how long are they kept, and what happens to an opted-in post that's later deleted?"** Today the posts never leave the phone in the demo: the Daily is made on-device from opted-in demo posts, and Muse is called with only the posts a maker picked. The server path (`POST /v1/generate`) takes a request and a brief, not your Group's posts, and has never completed a real end-to-end run. Retention, deletion and who-can-see rules for server-side generation are not designed yet; we'd tie them to the post's own audience and delete with it. That is a gap we name.

**6. "How much of the 5 minutes ran live versus frames, and which 'Muse-made' games did we see generated today?"** Everything on the phone is the real app on real Muse; the slides are slides; one moment (studio accounts and creator games) is said out loud as planned. The game built on stage is generated live; the seed games in the feed were written by our build agents, not Muse, and some still carry a "Made with Muse" stamp we are removing ([open-items.md](open-items.md#proposed-app-fixes)). Logged real Muse runs: the 10-game benchmark, 5 CLI samples, 2 in-app records, the 12 eval games and the fog pinball (D1, D10, D26, D30, D34).

**7. "Your own recommender sim raised pages per session. How is this not another engagement machine with a friendship label on it?"** Because what it rewards is a finished game, a save, a share and an Add friend, not time; quick skips count against a game; bait-averse people get a guard that holds their feed under 5 hooky games in 10 in 88.5% of windows (D28). The longer sessions in the sim are a side effect we'd watch as a guardrail, not a goal. And it is a simulation of synthetic people: it shows direction, not real behaviour. Our north star is new friendships and first Events, not minutes.

**8. "Ballpark: what does a Daily cost for 25 million Groups a day, and why no dollar figure?"** A formula game is 4 to 6 Muse calls and about 3,400 text output tokens (D9); a Daily is one game per Group, not per person; gameplay never calls a server. We never logged input tokens, and we haven't confirmed Muse prices on a Meta page, so we won't invent a number. Logging input tokens on the server's generate jobs is the first step.

### Where the IP line sits

- **What a person asks for is honored by name.** "A Star Wars game" gets Star Wars, Jedi and lightsabers in names, art and words. That is Karthik's decision (Nov 12), and the prompts on `main` say it (`d80ced9`, `df72dc3`, `07299ab`). Muse never adds a franchise the person didn't ask for.
- **Our own content stays original.** Seed games and the 16 gold examples use homages to genres (a magic school, a space rebellion), never a franchise's names, characters, places or quotes.
- **No copying of a specific game.** Mechanics stay original to the person's idea; no specific existing game's levels or art are copied; covers carry no text or logos.
- **No real people in games**, today. Muse's prompts ban real people and celebrities. The planned creator-inspired games are the one exception (below), and they are not built.
- **What's not solved.** At Meta scale, franchise requests would need rights checks, takedowns and possibly licensing, like any user content that names a brand. We have not built any of that. On stage, we don't demo a franchise game.

### Creators' names and likeness

**"You'd put MrBeast's name on a game he didn't make?"** Planned, not built. Karthik's call is to use real creators' names, labeled "A Curio inspired by MrBeast", never as a post *by* him, with original mechanics and no likeness (no face, voice or logo). Honestly, using a real person's name to promote a product raises right-of-publicity and endorsement questions, and at Meta the right version is a partnership: creators opt in, approve their game and share in what it earns, which is exactly the creator program Facebook already runs [27]. Until then we'd show it as a pitch example, and any real build would start with creators who agreed. (Our internal rule allows real names only for these accounts, as a logged exception to the synthetic-people rule.)

**"Why would creators want this?"** Their audience plays a game *together*, inside a Group, and every play is a reason to come back to Facebook. That is the "way back" in [01-story.md](01-story.md).

## Everything else

Questions are in rough order of likelihood. Each question appears once.

**"Why Facebook? Why can't another network do this as well?" (K)** The content is the people you know: a Group's posts, an Event's guest list, your friends. No viral game has ever used your friends as content. The games live where those people already are (Groups, Events, the feed, Reels, Stories, profiles, and chats once built), and every share lands back on Facebook. LinkedIn and the NYT show that games pull people into a bigger platform; here they pull people into Groups and friendships. Full answer: sections 3 and 7.

**"Why Facebook and not Instagram?"** Instagram reaches more young adults, but it has no Groups, no Events with RSVP lists and no Marketplace or local layer, and those are what the games are made from and played around. We would use Instagram Stories and WhatsApp as sharing channels (proposal, not built), and the sessions we add start from a Group, which Instagram doesn't host. See section 3.3.

**"Would this work without AI?"** No. Muse turns a described idea into a game with new mechanics, agrees how it works with the maker, writes it, repairs it, has bots play it, and draws its art (see [04-why-ai.md](04-why-ai.md)). A template can't write "gravity flips every tap". With the same prompts, our template composer made valid formula games, but one agent judged 6 of 10 off-topic (D7). And nobody can hand-write a daily game for every Group (VP-34).

**"Were those games really made by Muse?"** Yes, and the runs are logged. Our 10-run benchmark made 10 of 10 valid, bot-tested games with a median of 28.0 s (D1, D2). Earlier logged runs made Hill Drift (25.0 s), its night edit (40.6 s) and level 2 (25.4 s), First Hellos (39.4 s), Picnic Pals (33.0 s) (D10), and in the app a Send It restyle (15.8 s) and Send It 2 (28.4 s) (D26). Since then, the 12 eval games and the fog pinball were real Muse runs too (D30, D34). The 33 seed games in the app (including 8 microgames) are hand-made demo content written by our build agents, and 22 of them wrongly carry a "Made with Muse in N s" stamp (D39); we are removing those. We make one live on stage.

**"Muse takes half a minute or more. Won't people wait?"** A ready-formula game takes a median 28.0 s, and the game itself is ready at 21.0 s, before its cover (D2, D3). A game made from scratch takes a median 99 s (D30), and Studio shows Muse's real steps, with the bot lanes, the whole time. Studio shows each step live while the cover paints in parallel. Most people only play, and switching games takes about 3.6 ms on the simulator (D18, teammate-reported). Dailies are made ahead, and an offline composer covers outages.

**"Why a game at all? Admin AI could just write a welcome post or a 'meet who's coming' summary."** Meta already has Admin AI drafting posts in Forum [26], and a text summary is cheaper. A game does three things a text post can't:

- It gives a reason to return every day.
- It gives strangers a shared score, which is an excuse to introduce yourself.
- It gives lurkers a low-pressure role.

Lab work found "a social game can be more effective than a social task at fostering interpersonal trust" [72], which points the same way but is not proof. Section 11 proposes a direct test of a game icebreaker against an AI text icebreaker, measuring name recall and Add friend rate.

**"Does this bring people closer, or keep them on their phones?"** Every feature names who it brings closer: Add friend on board rows, icebreakers before the picnic, rematches in the group chat, games made together, Blends between two people. The feed ranks Add friend highest. The goal is walking into the picnic knowing three names. The pilot's north star is new friendships and first Events, not minutes (section 9).

**"Isn't competition the opposite of closer together?"** Not when it is friendly and fair. A score next to a stranger is a low-stakes reason to talk, and a rematch is a reason to message again. In a survey of 234 players, players who affected each other formed ties whether or not they shared a goal, and toxicity cut ties [72]. Competition between groups with prizes can divide them [130], so boards stay scoped to your Group, Event or friends, everyone plays the same seed, Blends add a plus-only bonus for evenly matched games (not yet in the app), and weekly boards add Most improved. See "Why friendly competition makes friends".

**"Isn't an endless game feed another engagement machine?"** A fair tension our design review raised. Daily play is capped at one scored run, the result sheet ends in a person, and ranking rewards connection outcomes such as Add friend and shares to a Group, while quick skips count against (VP-10). In an earlier simulation of synthetic users, bait-averse people saw clickbait fall from 36% to 14%, against 49% when ranking on clicks alone (D21; simulated, teammate-reported). The current recommender's guard keeps bait-averse people under 5 hooky games per 10 pages in 88.5% of windows (D28, simulated). We would measure social actions, not minutes; see hard question 7 above.

**"Games built from my posts? I didn't agree to be a puzzle answer."** Only posts their authors marked for Muse Curios are read, the maker can drop any, and the timeline names what was used and says nothing else was read (VP-21). Muse is told never to invent facts about people, and the data stays with Meta's own models. Two gaps are real and named: there is no opt-in toggle in the post composer yet (opt-ins are set in demo data), and no separate moderation step.

**"Can someone make a game to mock a person?"** Yes, unless the design stops it, and integrity matters as much as privacy here. Our design rules:

- Games about a named person must be made by that person, or need that person's consent (not built).
- Anyone in a game gets a "remove me from this game" action (not built).
- Group admins review Group-sourced games before they run (not built).
- Meta's existing bullying and harassment classifiers would apply to briefs and content (not built).

What exists today: only opted-in posts are read, prompts tell Muse to keep content kind and never invent facts, and the maker can drop any source. Until the rest is built, games about people stay limited to opted-in intros in the demo world.

**"Isn't this AI slop? Who moderates?"** Every Curio has a human maker, a named audience and remix credits (VP-37). Games must validate and pass a 5-bot playtest before anyone sees them (D5). Prompts forbid invented facts and require kind content. A moderation step and admin approval for Group Dailies are required before launch, and we list them as not built.

**"What if Muse makes a broken game?"** A formula game never ships broken. Every draft is validated and played by 5 bots in the real engine, with up to 2 repairs. In the benchmark 9 of 10 first drafts passed and the tenth was fixed in one turn (D5); 3 of the 5 earlier CLI runs were caught and fixed (D10). A game that fails to load shows "This Curio didn't load" instead of a blank screen. For games made from scratch, there is one honest exception: if neither the draft nor its last repair passes the gate, Studio keeps the one with fewer blocking problems and shows the gate's report, rather than nothing. In the eval baseline passed the gate 12 of 12, with 75% of first drafts valid and 0.3 repairs on average (D30).

**"Who makes all these Dailies? Most people don't create. Will people actually make games?"** They don't have to. The supply is Muse: an admin turns on the Daily once, Muse generates it each day from opted-in posts, and the admin can preview and veto (VP-18; admin opt-in not built). Event icebreakers are made the same way. Members' creations are a bonus, and making one takes a sentence, with starter prompts and "Surprise me". Admin opt-in is the key hypothesis, and the pilot measures it.

**"Can't people cheat the leaderboard?"** Every run is a seed plus an input log. Re-simulating it checks the score; that costs about 4.06 ms of CPU per run in Node, and 390 of 390 bot runs reproduced exactly (D12, D14; bot-run). The board marks verified runs, and challenges use the same seed, so they're fair by construction. Verification proves the inputs produce the score, not that a human entered them; bot-input detection is a later server-side layer (VP-16).

**"Facebook already tried games. Why will this work?"** Earlier attempts were studio catalogs bolted on: FarmVille's request spam led to notification limits [39], Instant Games left Messenger on iOS in 2019 [36], and the Gaming app closed in 2022 [37]. Meta still says hundreds of millions of people play games and join gaming Groups in the main app each month [37]. Muse Curios is made by members, about the Group, inside Groups and Events, with scoped invites only and no mass requests. It is also the format Meta's CEO says is next [1].

**"Isn't this just Pocket, or YouTube Playables?"** Those make games for a feed of strangers or a creator's audience [43][101]. Muse Curios makes games from and for a specific Group, Event or set of friends, using data rivals don't have (VP-28).

**"Isn't this just Wordle or Flappy Bird?"** The mechanics are familiar on purpose; the content is the people you know. Wordle is one global puzzle, and its plays fell from 5.3 billion in 2024 to 4.2 billion in 2025 [83]; Muse Curios is a new daily game per community, made from that community. Muse is told never to copy a specific game's levels or art; a theme or franchise the person asks for is honored by name (see [where the IP line sits](#where-the-ip-line-sits)). Prior art such as PuzzleScript and VGDL already describes small games as data; what's new is AI-made content scoped to your Group.

**"Isn't Facebook for older people?"** 69% of US adults aged 18 to 29 use Facebook (2026) [52], and 49% of all 18-to-29-year-olds use it daily (2025) [53]. We don't claim it is their favorite app: YouTube and Instagram reach more [52]. Our claim is that the reach exists and the gap is a daily reason to come back. LinkedIn shows daily games can work on a network young people don't open for fun [79][81].

**"A newcomer has no friends there. Doesn't the friend graph do nothing for them?"** Mostly true at first, and we say so ([05-why-facebook.md](05-why-facebook.md)). For them, the moat is Groups and Events: a chosen, shared context plus everyday names. The friend graph matters for their hometown friends (VP-14), and it grows as they add people from boards and Blends.

**"Blends sounds like matchmaking. Is it?"** No. The Group Blend in the app picks games for everyone in a Group, and offers a "taste twin": a member of a Group you both joined who plays what you play, with Add friend. That is a suggestion of someone in a shared Group, the same scope as a board row, not of strangers at large. The planned two-person Blend is a game for two people who have already met, and a bracket is for people at the same Event (VP-40). Dating is out of scope ([12-rubric-and-brief.md](12-rubric-and-brief.md)).

**"What does a game cost, and how does it scale?"** 4 to 6 Muse calls and a median of about 3,400 text output tokens (D9); verification is milliseconds (D14). A Daily is one game per Group, not per person. A game is a small JSON file, checked once at publish, then cached and played on the phone, so gameplay never calls a server. We have not logged input tokens, so we state no dollar figure (see hard question 8). Games made from scratch cost more: a median 99 s of Muse time and up to 3 repair rounds; we have not counted their tokens.

**"What about cold start: an empty Group, few opt-ins, empty boards?"** A Daily needed only 5 opted-in posts (D8). If there are none, members can still make games from a sentence, and the offline composer means a game always comes out. The Daily card invites the first player to be first on the board. In production, empty boards would show "be first", never fake people. The server already holds real data only (the demo world was removed in `0636df3`), and the planned Meta studio accounts would post a new game every day so no feed starts empty ([11-roadmap.md](11-roadmap.md)).

**"Is there a backend? Are those scores real?"** Yes, a backend is live: Max's Go and MongoDB servers run on Prod, with auth, the recommender (Kevin's formula running unchanged in Go, matching Node in a golden test), a ranked feed, search, Blend and a generate endpoint that holds the Muse key (D36). The demo itself runs on the phone, with the app's mock API by default, and other people's activity is simulated, but the Muse calls are real. Board rows come from bots playing the real engine (Maya plays at skill 0.9), so each score has a replay. Server generation has never completed a real end-to-end run. Today's Daily in the app is a hand-made seed, or made with Studio's daily mode through a debug launch.

**"How does Meta make money?"** Not built, and the honest answer is indirect: return visits and Group activity on surfaces that already carry ads. Options from the games we studied: ads between runs, cosmetics, a games subscription like NYT's, themed packs; sponsored Muse Curios for Groups and Events are a possible later format (VP-31).

**"Can an iOS app even run these games?"** App Store guideline 4.7 allows HTML5 and JavaScript mini games inside apps, with content filtering, reporting, blocking, an index of the software and an age gate [124]. Muse Curios are manifests run by a fixed, built-in runtime, so they fall under that rule. Reporting, the index and the age gate are not built.

**"Why build your own engine? Why JavaScript?"** Replays, Bursts and score checks need a fixed clock we control, and Studio runs the same code headless to playtest. The formula games run on a light JavaScript runtime. Games made from scratch run on Artem's Rust/WASM engine (v0.2: assets, physics, typing, audio, verifiable replays; 200 tests), which is in the app side by side with the runtime: about 1.5 MB, and 8 to 15 ms added to the first microgame.

**"What about teens?"** Muse Curios is designed for adults. Meta limits Muse to people 18 and over [6], and its reported 2026 settlement with attorneys general on teen mental health [6] (we have not checked the terms ourselves; don't quote the number on stage) argues for adults-first, opt-in, finite-by-default design.

**Also ready if asked.**

- **How did a small team build this much?** Each of us ran AI coding agents in parallel, each owning its own files, coordinated through a shared spec, Slack canvases and CLAUDE.md. Whether to mention it is open ([open-items.md](open-items.md)). Don't claim "no merge conflicts": the integration merges needed fixes (for example `6b1cc85`).
- **What was hard about Muse?** The text models reason first, so a request needs room for 4,000 or more output tokens or the reply comes back empty.
- **What's next?** See [11-roadmap.md](11-roadmap.md). Pick from: Meta studio accounts and creator-inspired games; a genre-aware gate so calm and puzzle games pass; challenges that cross phones; automatic daily generation on a server with replay-checked boards; Blends and brackets; photos in requests via Spark; spoken requests via Muse voice transcription; sketch input and play insights; new mechanics as sandboxed plugins; Ember re-ranking the feed with a one-line "why".
- **Why this need?** Our first concept served existing roommates, which none of Meta's four needs cover. A new city is where Groups and Events matter most.
- **Accessibility?** High contrast, reduced motion, one-handed play and screen-reader labels are in the design, not yet audited.
