Short, repeatable games are among the most common ways young adults play, but they are easy to drop, so sharing and a light daily ritual matter as much as the game itself.

- **Almost everyone plays, mostly on phones.** 98% of US 13–39-year-olds play some kind of game, 75% of Gen Z play on mobile, and casual mobile is the most-played genre among 18–25-year-olds.
- **Sessions are short.** A typical mobile session lasts 7–8 minutes and a hyper-casual one under 3; play fills waits, commutes and the minutes before sleep.
- **The pull is psychological, not just habit.** Quick proof of competence, bursts of flow, relief from boredom and stress, and small shared rituals explain why people come back.
- **Sharing drives growth.** Wordle went from under 100 to millions of daily players in about three months on shared results; comparing scores predicts continued play.
- **Churn and manipulation are the risks.** Hyper-casual games keep about 3% of players by day 30, and dark patterns are common even in harmless-looking games.
- **For Bubbles:** keep rounds tiny, make results shareable, favour friend challenges and daily prompts, and skip manipulative hooks.

## Scope and method

This review covers games whose single round lasts from a few seconds to about 10 minutes and that invite another round right away or the next day. That includes daily puzzles, hyper-casual and casual mobile games, arcade score-chasers and in-feed or in-chat games. "Young adults" means roughly 18 to 29; where a source reports Gen Z (born about 1997 to 2012) or 18 to 34, that is noted.

Sources fall into three tiers, and the review weighs them in this order:

1. Peer-reviewed studies: motivation research, wellbeing studies built on play logs, and randomized trials.
2. Large representative surveys: Pew Research Center and the Entertainment Software Association (ESA).
3. Industry telemetry and vendor surveys: GameAnalytics, Newzoo, GWI and others. These have big samples but are run by firms with a stake in the result.

The search was a targeted web and literature search done on 13 November 2026, not a systematic review. Few studies isolate both short games and the 18 to 29 group, so most findings below are pieced together from sources that cover one or the other.

## How many young adults play

Nearly all young adults play some kind of game, mostly on phones, but only a minority call themselves "gamers". That gap is where short, casual games live.

| Finding | Group | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 98% play video games in some form | US, ages 13–39 | YPulse, 2026 |
| 60% play often or sometimes (72% of men, 49% of women) | US, ages 18–29 | Pew Research Center, 2017 |
| 33% of men and 9% of women say "gamer" describes them well | US, ages 18–29 | Pew Research Center, 2015 |
| 60% of adults play every week; 79% of Gen Z play with friends | US, ages 18+ (n = 5,000) | ESA Essential Facts, 2025 |
| 75% play on mobile; 9 h 11 min of play a week | Gen Z, global | GWI and Gameloft, 2026 |
| Casual mobile is the most-played genre | US, ages 18–25 (n > 2,000) | ZBD via GamesBeat, 2024 |
| 3.0 billion mobile players, 83% of all players | Global, all ages | Newzoo via PocketGamer.biz, 2025 |

Three points stand out. First, play is close to universal, so the question is what format young adults pick, not whether they play. Second, the phone is the default device. Third, the gender gap shrinks sharply once casual and mobile play are counted: Pew's 23-point gap in who plays turns into a much larger gap in who claims the "gamer" label. Short games reach the people who play but don't see themselves as gamers.

The weak spot is age detail. Pew's most recent 18–29 play figure is from 2017, and the ESA does not publish a weekly-play rate for Gen Z or millennials.

## Session length and play patterns

Mobile sessions are short: a typical one lasts 7 to 8 minutes, and hyper-casual sessions average under 3 minutes.

The length tracks the game type. Games with a natural end to each round (a match, a puzzle, a run) stay short, while casino, card and role-playing games with long-running goals reach 13 to 22 minutes. GameAnalytics also found the industry average session fell by about a minute from 2018 to 2019.

Short games are also played in predictable gaps in the day:

- **Daily puzzles:** in a 2022 survey of 1,087 US Wordle players (average age 36), most solved it in under 10 minutes, and more than half played on waking or before sleep (Solitaired). This is a company survey, not academic work.
- **In-between moments:** university students tend to reach for mobile games when bored in their free time (Öz and Üstün, 2022), which fits short play between other activities.

No source found here reports session length broken out by age, so the link between short sessions and young adults rests on platform data (they play mostly on phones) rather than on direct measurement.

## Case studies

The best-known short games spread through sharing and friend challenges, not marketing, and the ones that lasted added a limit or a reason to come back.

| Case | What happened | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Wordle (2021–22) | Under 100 daily players on 1 Nov 2021, about 300,000 a few weeks later, millions daily by the end of January 2022. The New York Times bought it for a price "in the low seven figures". | One puzzle a day, the same for everyone, plus a spoiler-free emoji grid to share. Growth came from shared results. |
| Flappy Bird (2013–14) | Over 50 million App Store downloads. The developer said it earned about $50,000 a day from ads, then pulled it on 10 February 2014. | One-tap control, runs of a few seconds, instant restart. A hard, simple loop can spread on its own. |
| Facebook Instant Games (2016) | Launched 30 Nov 2016 with 17 HTML5 games in 30 countries, playable in Messenger and News Feed with no install. | Meta's own earlier bet: play inside the feed and chat, with leaderboards and challenges sent to friends. |
| Hyper-casual mobile (2017–present) | Sessions average about 170 seconds; about 3% of players remain at day 30, against 8–11% for sports, racing and tabletop games (Appodeal, 2025). | Very short loops win installs but lose players fast. The industry is moving to "hybrid casual" games with progress that carries over. |

The pattern across the four: a round that takes seconds to learn, a result worth showing someone, and almost no friction to start. Wordle's daily limit is the outlier that also fixed the churn problem, because scarcity turned a quick game into a daily habit.

## Why they appeal

The research points to four reasons short games pull people back: they make players feel competent fast, they fill spare moments, they ease stress, and they create small shared rituals.

- **Competence, autonomy and relatedness.** Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski ran four studies with about 1,000 players and found that a game's pull comes from meeting these three needs, not from "shallow fun" (University of Rochester, 2006; Motivation and Emotion). A short round with a clear score gives quick, repeated proof of skill. Players also choose when to play, which supports autonomy.
- **Flow in small doses.** A game matched to the player's skill produces flow and positive mood. In a Candy Crush experiment with 60 players, Larche and Dixon found that people who are often bored got more absorbed and felt better while playing (University of Waterloo, 2021; Computers in Human Behavior).
- **Filling gaps and beating boredom.** In a 2022 study of 552 Turkish university students (average age 21.8), leisure boredom explained 33% of the variation in motivation to play mobile games (Öz and Üstün, 2022).
- **Stress relief.** In a six-month randomized trial, Bejeweled 2 cut physiological stress (measured by heart-rate variability) by 54% against a control group (East Carolina University, 2008). The game's publisher funded the study, and the two other games tested did not lower physical stress.
- **Insight, scarcity and ritual.** Social psychologist Matt Baldwin attributes Wordle's pull to the jolt of insight when the answer appears, a challenge that doesn't demand much attention, and a one-a-day limit that keeps it fresh (University of Florida, 2022). This is expert commentary, not a study.

These games also reach people outside the core audience. Jesper Juul's *A Casual Revolution* (MIT Press) argues casual games redefined who counts as a player, which fits the gap between who plays and who calls themselves a gamer.

On wellbeing more broadly, the largest log-based study so far tracked about 39,000 adults over six weeks and found little to no causal link between time spent playing and wellbeing (Vuorre et al., 2022; Royal Society Open Science). The authors say why people play matters more than how long.

## Social play and sharing

For young adults, games are social: 79% of Gen Z play with friends, and 89% say games can help them make new friends (ESA, 2025). Short games turn that into comparing scores rather than playing together live.

- **Comparing keeps people playing.** In a study of online social games, Esteves, Valogianni and Greenhill found that two kinds of comparison best predicted continued play: identifying with players doing better, and contrasting yourself with players doing worse (Information & Management, 2021).
- **Sharing signals belonging.** Baldwin links Wordle's share feature to "shared reality": a preference feels more valid when others share it, and posting a result marks you as part of the group (Newswise, 2022). He also notes the downside: comparing up to people who always do better can hurt self-esteem.
- **Sharing is a minority act with a big reach.** Only about 1 in 4 Wordle players share scores consistently, mostly by text to friends and family, while about half compete with friends for bragging rights (Solitaired, 2022). A few sharers can spread a game to many non-sharers.
- **Challenges inside chat.** Facebook's 2016 Instant Games put leaderboards and friend challenges into Messenger group threads and kept scores visible only to the people a player was playing with (Meta for Developers).

The evidence here is thinner than for motivation. No study found tests friends-only, take-your-turn leaderboards on retention in young adults directly.

## Risks and limits

Short games are easy to start and just as easy to drop, and the tricks used to bring players back can tip into manipulation.

- **Churn is steep.** The median mobile game keeps about 25% of players on day 1 (GameAnalytics, 2019). Hyper-casual games keep about 3% by day 30, against 8–11% for sports, racing and tabletop games (Appodeal, 2025). A fun loop alone does not make a habit.
- **Escape play can become a problem.** Larche and Dixon warn that players who use games to escape boredom may play more often and longer, which can crowd out other activities and may worsen low mood (University of Waterloo, 2021). They suggest play-time limits as a safeguard.
- **Dark patterns are common.** An analysis of user data on 1,496 mobile games found manipulative design (pressure on time, money, social ties and psychology) even in games that look harmless (Niknejad et al., 2024).
- **Young players see the tricks but accept them.** In a survey of 1,000 people aged 16 to 25, 92.7% played regularly, 70.9% mostly on phones, and about six in ten spent money each month. Many treated monetization as normal; the authors flag chance-based rewards, virtual currencies and penalties for non-payers as the most harmful (Universitat Oberta de Catalunya, 2026; Entertainment Computing).

Gaps in the evidence:

- Few studies split results by age, so young adults are often inferred from "Gen Z" or "mobile" data.
- Much of the session and retention data comes from vendors with an interest in the result, and some is several years old.
- The stress-relief trial was funded by the games' publisher.
- Wordle findings rest on expert commentary and a company survey, not peer-reviewed research.

## Do games drive people to the rest of the app?

LinkedIn and The New York Times both say their games keep users coming back. Neither has published numbers showing that games send people to other parts of the app, and the one independent measurement found a small, short-lived effect.

| Case | What the company says | Independent evidence |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn Games (2024–) | 84% of players come back the next day and 80% within a week (Nicholas Pezarro, games product manager). Daily players are "more likely to consume other content," such as feed posts, news and sponsored posts. No figures given. | None found. |
| NYT Games (2022–) | Subscribers who use both news and games in a week keep their subscriptions longest (Jonathan Knight, head of games). Wordle brought in "tens of millions" of new users, and new buyers are steered to the news-and-games bundle. No figures given. | Datos clickstream, May 2023–Apr 2024: among people visiting NYT games for the first time, 15.3% read NYT news in the month before, 22.4% in the month after and 16% the month after that. |

How LinkedIn set this up: leaderboards rank you against your connections, colleagues and alumni, and posting a result to the feed is optional (Fortune, 2024). Knight says Wordle brought in a younger, more diverse and more international audience than the news side has (Aftermath, 2024).

What this means: the proven benefit is a daily habit and retention, not sending players elsewhere. People who play daily seem to stay engaged across the app. But a game alone has not been shown to lead players into the core product for long.

## Implications for Bubbles

The evidence supports Bubbles' core bet of tiny games inside Facebook, with three conditions: rounds must be very short, results must be easy to share, and there has to be a reason to come back that isn't a manipulative hook.

| Lesson | Evidence | What it means for Bubbles |
|---|---|---|
| Keep a round under about 3 minutes | Hyper-casual sessions average about 170 s; play fills gaps in the day | Every Bubble should be playable in one short gap, with an instant restart |
| Make results shareable without spoilers | Wordle's emoji grid; comparison predicts continued play | A results card that posts to the feed or a chat, showing how you did, not the answer |
| Prefer challenges between friends to global rankings | 79% of Gen Z play with friends; comparing upward can hurt | Friends-only leaderboards and "beat my score" challenges in Messenger and Groups |
| Add a light reason to return | Hyper-casual keeps about 3% at day 30; Wordle's daily limit built a habit | Daily or shared prompts (one challenge a day per Group), not energy timers |
| Make sure players feel more skilled each round | Competence predicts enjoyment and future play (Ryan et al., 2006) | Fair difficulty, clear scoring and quick feedback in what Muse generates |
| Leave out dark patterns | Found widely, even in harmless-looking games; young players accept them as normal | No loot boxes, virtual currencies or loss-based streak pressure; consider an optional play-time limit |

One open question for the team: no study here measures how creating a game, rather than playing one, affects engagement. That is the new part of Bubbles and would need its own testing.
