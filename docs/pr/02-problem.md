# The problem

*The problem, who has it and why it is not solved today. Part of the pitch docs; start at [README.md](README.md).*

## The problem and the solution, in short

*How this fits the story.* The lead problem, in [01-story.md](01-story.md), is that Facebook doesn't appeal to young adults: their friends spend their time on Instagram, so nothing pulls them back. Everything below is the detail. The newcomer in a new city is the sharpest case, and the one the demo follows, because a person who just moved needs people *and* has accounts on Facebook already.

**Problem.** Young adults still have Facebook accounts, and many of their Groups and Events live there, but Facebook has stopped giving them a reason to open it, or a reason to keep opening it. Their friends' attention has moved elsewhere: 44% of 18-to-29-year-old users deleted the app from their phone in a single year (2018) [132], and the number of peers on a network is one of the strongest drivers of staying on it [131]. Teen use fell from 71% in 2014-15 to 32% in 2024 [54], and daily use now peaks among people aged 30 to 64 [53]. Their friends, Groups and Events are all there, but there is little to *do* with them, so a visit rarely becomes a habit.

**Solution.** Other apps and news sites keep people coming back with small daily rituals: a streak, a daily puzzle, one game everyone in the chat talks about. We took that idea and made it collaborative. Whims is a space inside Facebook where anyone turns one sentence into a tiny game with Meta's Muse models, made from and for their own Group, Event or friends, and played together on the same seed. Making one is a reason to open Facebook. A new Daily, a friend's challenge and a rematch are reasons to come back. And because the game is about your people, a score next to a stranger becomes an introduction.

### Who has the problem

Young adults, roughly 18 to 29, whose people are on Facebook but who have no reason to open it. The need is sharpest in the middle of a change (a move, a first job, a new campus), when a reason to show up matters most. Three situations show it:

- **A new city.** Alex moved to San Francisco two weeks ago. They joined New in SF and RSVP'd to a picnic, and they know nobody in either.
- **A quiet group chat.** Their hometown friends' chat used to be busy. Now it is a birthday message every few months.
- **A Group that only posts announcements.** Their running club's Group posts the Saturday route and nothing else. Members show up, run and leave.

The newcomer is the sharpest case, and the one our demo follows. The same gap exists for anyone whose people are on Facebook but have nothing to do together there, and Whims is built so anyone can make a game for any of their people.

### The problem, in detail

1. **Facebook has the people but no longer the reason.** 69% of US adults aged 18 to 29 use Facebook [52] and 49% of all 18-to-29-year-olds use it daily, yet Facebook's daily use peaks among people aged 30 to 64 [53]. Teen use has collapsed, from 71% in 2014-15 to 32% in 2024 [54], so the next generation of young adults is arriving without the habit.
2. **People come back for rituals, and the best rituals are shared.** LinkedIn reports "86% of players returning the next day and 82% still playing after seven" for its daily games [79]. Duolingo learners who reach a 7-day streak are 3.6 times more likely to complete their course [114]. But a single global puzzle fades: Wordle fell from 5.3 billion plays in 2024 to 4.2 billion in 2025 [83], and the NYT responded with a friends tab "to encourage communal play" [84]. The ritual that lasts is the one you share with your people.
3. **Facebook's rooms have nothing to do in them.** In a Group or Event of strangers, the only ways to take part are to write an intro or comment, or to lurk. People underestimate how much new acquaintances like them, which "can hinder our ability to develop new relationships" [68].
4. **The people who most need a reason to show up are starting over.** Ages 19 to 29 are the loneliest age group worldwide: 27% very or fairly lonely, against 17% of those 65 and over [60]. 26.6% of 18-to-24-year-olds changed address in the past year (2023, including local moves) [63]. Friendship takes about 50 hours to become casual friends, 90 to become friends and more than 200 to become close friends [65], and in-person time with friends for ages 15 to 24 fell from about 150 to 40 minutes a day between 2003 and 2020 [59]. Meta itself names the moment: "moving, going to college, getting their first job or apartment" [13].
5. **Group chats and plans stall.** Messenger saw 2.5 million new group chats a day in 2017 (latest public figure) [19], but a chat with nothing to do goes quiet, and "we should hang out" rarely becomes a plan. A shared daily game can restart one: Wordle revived a quiet family group chat [88].

### Why it isn't solved today

- **Facebook has the rooms, not the activity.** Groups, Events and friends all live on Facebook, used by almost 1.8 billion people a month in Groups alone [14]. What a newcomer can do there is post, comment or scroll. Facebook's past games did not fix this: Instant Games needed studios to make each game [34], and FarmVille's growth came from notifications Facebook later limited as spam [39].
- **Event apps cover only the meetup.** Partiful [122] and Apple Invites [121] have guest lists, but no standing community before or after the Event.
- **Community apps lack everyday identity.** Discord has servers, events and live games, but pseudonymous accounts; it added age checks only in 2026 [99]. Meeting a stranger there starts without Facebook's everyday-name norm [30].
- **AI game makers have no social context.** YouTube's Playables Builder [101], Roblox's 4D generation [96] and Meta's own Pocket [44] make games from prompts, but the games flow from a creator to an audience. None puts a game inside a Group, an Event or a chat, made about the people in it.
- **Daily games are the same for everyone.** LinkedIn's games [79] and NYT Games [82] build a daily habit, but with one editorial puzzle for the whole world, not one about your Group.

### Where this came from

Karthik's first brainstorm was a playable layer across Facebook: tiny games anyone makes with Muse, shown in the feed, on Stories and profiles, with leaderboards, remixes and linked levels, and a look as distinct as the Muse brand. The team's problem statement asked which young-adult need it served and why someone would open Facebook again, and Bayan proposed tying it to making friends in a new city. After a judge's feedback on 2026-11-12, Karthik made "closer together" the top priority: every feature has to bring specific people closer through Facebook's own surfaces, by friendly competition, shared stories and self-expression, and Kevin and Karthik's Blends idea came from that turn. Karthik then sharpened the problem to Facebook itself: people need a reason to be on Facebook and a reason to stay, and Whims answers both with a collaborative take on the daily rituals of other social apps and news sites. This section states that current framing.
